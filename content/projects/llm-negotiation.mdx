<Lead>
NegotiationArena (Bianchi et al., ICML 2024) evaluates the negotiation abilities of LLM-based agents, but its original evaluation was limited to proprietary models, some of which have since been deprecated. This project evaluates nine open-weight models in the same three scenarios and analyses the impact of two inference-time techniques: Self-Refine and team negotiation.
</Lead>

## Open-Weight Benchmark

The benchmark follows the experimental framework of NegotiationArena, replacing its proprietary agents with open-weight models from the Gemma, Mistral, and Qwen families. The models are grouped into comparable 4–9B, 12–14B, and 24–27B parameter tiers. Each ordered cross-play pairing is run 30 times in every game; because the games are role-asymmetric, both orderings are evaluated.

In each scenario, two agents alternate turns until one side terminates the game or the turn budget is exhausted. A move must be expressed with structured XML-like tags inside the response. Performance is measured using **win rate**, **average payoff**, and **completion rate**: the proportion of games that reach a valid final state.

### Multi-Turn Ultimatum Game

Player 1 begins with all 100 ZUP and must propose a split for Player 2. A proposal only has value if the other party accepts it: an outright rejection leaves both parties with nothing. The responder may also make a counter-proposal, so the game can continue across several turns. The party with the higher share of an agreed split wins.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/ultimatum-game.png" alt="Diagram of the multi-turn Ultimatum game, showing proposal, acceptance, counter-proposal, and rejection outcomes" caption="Multi-turn Ultimatum: a proposed split only has value if the responder accepts it." />

### Trading Game

The parties begin with complementary resource holdings: Player 1 has 25 X and 5 Y, while Player 2 has 5 X and 25 Y. Since X and Y have the same value and each party starts with 30 units in total, a completed trade is zero-sum: one party's net gain is the other's loss. On each turn, an agent can propose a trade, accept the standing offer, or wait for a better one. The agent with the larger net increase in resources wins.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/trading-game.png" alt="Diagram of the Trading game, showing asymmetric starting resources and alternating trade proposals" caption="Trading: each party begins with a different resource mix and negotiates an exchange." />

### BuySell

BuySell is an incomplete-information price negotiation. The seller knows the object costs 40 ZUP to produce; the buyer knows it is worth 60 ZUP to them. Any agreed price between 40 and 60 creates value, but the price decides how the resulting 20-ZUP surplus is divided: seller payoff is <code>price − 40</code>, while buyer payoff is <code>60 − price</code>. The agents can make an offer, accept the standing offer, or reject it and end the game with no deal.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/buysell-game.png" alt="Diagram of the BuySell game, showing the seller's cost, buyer's valuation, and price-negotiation outcomes" caption="BuySell: the agreed price divides the surplus between the seller's private cost and the buyer's private valuation." />

### Completion and the Parse-Error Retry Loop

A practical obstacle with open-weight models was that they could violate the required tag format, leaving a response unparseable and ending the game prematurely. I therefore evaluated a parse-error retry loop: when a move fails to parse, the agent receives the invalid response and parser error, then has up to three attempts to resubmit it in the required format.

Of the 9.7% of moves that triggered the loop, 75% were corrected on the first retry and 93% within the three-retry budget. The loop recovered lost games without materially changing the model ranking (Spearman correlation between win rates with and without retries: 0.92).

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/retried-moves-resolution.png" alt="Breakdown of parse-error retry outcomes" caption="Most malformed moves are corrected on the first retry; 93% are resolved within the three-retry budget." />

### Model-Family Performance

Qwen is the most consistent family, winning 63.0%, 63.4%, and 66.8% of decisive games at the very small, small, and medium tiers, respectively. Gemma follows at 44.5–45.3%, and Mistral at 38.0–42.8%. This pooled ranking hides meaningful differences between games: Qwen is strongest in BuySell at every tier and in Trading at the small and medium tiers, while Gemma is strongest in Ultimatum.

The role asymmetries reported for proprietary models also appear here. Being Player 2 increases win rate by 47.7 percentage points in Trading and 53.9 in BuySell; in Ultimatum the direction reverses, with the proposer holding a strong advantage.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/role-effect-family-seat.png" alt="Win rate by model family and player seat across the three games" caption="Role effects by family and seat. Trading and BuySell favour Player 2, whereas Ultimatum strongly favours the first mover." />

In BuySell, the final price is strongly associated with the seller's opening price (correlation 0.752), reproducing the anchoring effect reported in NegotiationArena. The open-weight models also exhibit irrational behaviour: sellers open below their production cost in 16.2% of games, and 12.9% of completed deals close below cost.

## Social Personas

Following NegotiationArena, I tested whether a short behavioural instruction changes the outcome of a negotiation. Player 1 always receives the default game prompt. Player 2 receives either no additional instruction, a desperate persona, or a cunning persona. The experiment is run in self-play, so any difference comes from the persona and the response to it, rather than a capability difference between the parties.

The persona text is appended directly to Player 2's system prompt:

The desperate and cunning personas that improved GPT-4's outcomes in NegotiationArena do not transfer consistently to open-weight models. Their effect depends on the game and the model. In Ultimatum, cunning raises the responder's win rate from 14.3% to 70.5%, but raises the no-deal rate to 73% and produces the lowest mean payoff of the conditions. In BuySell, desperate raises the buyer's win rate from 75.2% to 96.7%; in Trading, both personas reduce it.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/persona-winrate-by-game.png" alt="Player 2 win rate by persona across Trading, Ultimatum, and BuySell" caption="Player 2 win rate by persona and game. Cunning gives the largest gain in Ultimatum and desperate the largest in BuySell, but neither improves Trading." />

The effect also differs sharply by model. Cunning in Ultimatum raises the win rate of Gemma 3 27B by 0.90 and Qwen3.5 27B by 0.80, but lowers it by 0.47 for Ministral 3 8B. Personas therefore do not create a consistent capability gain; they amplify a style whose value depends on both the game and the model carrying it.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/persona-effect-spread.png" alt="Change in Player 2 win rate by persona and model, relative to the default condition" caption="Per-model change in Player 2 win rate relative to the default condition. The spread is particularly wide for the cunning persona." />

Win rate is misleading here because it excludes ties, including no-deals where both parties receive zero. Cunning raises the no-deal rate in every game, reaching 73% in Ultimatum compared with 13% under the default condition. Despite its high win rate, it produces the lowest mean Ultimatum payoff: $16.5 compared with $26.2 at default. When cunning deals do close, however, the responder keeps $61.7 on average, compared with $30.2 at default.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/persona-nodeal-by-game.png" alt="No-deal rate by persona across Trading, Ultimatum, and BuySell" caption="No-deal rate by persona and game. Cunning raises it in every game and sends most Ultimatum games to a destroyed pot." />

Cunning is therefore high-risk, high-reward. It frequently causes the responder to reject and destroy the pot, but when it closes a deal it can extract an unusually large share. The following run illustrates the latter case: the persona escalates from a 50-dollar counteroffer to 95 dollars, and the default proposer eventually accepts.

## Self-Refine

Before committing a move, the agent produces an initial draft, critiques it along five negotiation-specific criteria—format, payoff alignment, opponent plausibility, consistency, and rule compliance—and rewrites it. The loop runs twice; only the final response enters the public game history.

Self-Refine does not provide clear general improvements. Its effect is asymmetric: it usually helps a structurally disadvantaged party but tends to penalise one that is already strong. When applied to disadvantaged seats, win rate improves in 7 of 9 game-by-tier cells, by 5.6 percentage points on average; for advantaged seats, the mean change is −2.8 points. Only 5 of the 18 cells are statistically significant.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/refine-impact-heatmap.png" alt="Self-Refine effect on win rate and payoff by game, seat, and parameter tier" caption="Effect of refining one seat relative to the default baseline. The structurally disadvantaged seats tend to gain, while already strong seats tend to lose; most cells are indistinguishable from zero." />

To understand how refinement changes proposals, each revision is labelled by the payoff the agent would receive if the proposal were accepted. A revision hardens when it claims more for the agent and softens when it claims less. Where a proposal changes, hardening is more common than softening, particularly in Trading.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/refine-revision-direction.png" alt="Direction of Self-Refine proposal revisions by game and parameter tier" caption="Direction of revisions between the initial draft and committed move. Where the offer changes, hardening is generally more common than softening." />

Because the refinement loop produces a draft, two critiques, and two rewrites, it generates roughly six times as much text per move as the default strategy: a median of 4,900 characters against 790. It modestly improves completion, but the gains in negotiation outcomes do not justify that additional cost in general.

## Team Negotiation

One negotiating party becomes a private team of three models. Members draft independently, revise over two discussion rounds while seeing each other's drafts, then rank the slate. A Borda count picks the committed move, and the rationale is rewritten in first person so the opponent never sees the team.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/team.webp" alt="Team deliberation protocol" caption="Independent drafts, discussion rounds, Borda consensus." />

The team experiments compare homogeneous trios with heterogeneous teams containing one Gemma, one Mistral, and one Qwen model. Deliberation does not produce a systematic win-rate improvement: no team-versus-single-agent comparison separates from its matched baseline. Some localised payoff gains appear, particularly in structurally weak seats, but the effect is not general.

Heterogeneous teams produce more diverse drafts than homogeneous teams (mean pairwise cosine distance of approximately 0.50 versus 0.36), yet this diversity is not positively associated with payoff or win rate. Across 650 committed heterogeneous moves, the Mistral member's draft wins the Borda count 80.5% of the time, even though Mistral is the weakest family in the single-agent benchmark.

<Figure src="/projects/llm-negotiation/hetero-family-consensus-authorship.png" alt="Share of heterogeneous-team committed moves authored by each model family" caption="Chosen-move authorship in heterogeneous teams. Mistral accounts for about 80% of committed moves across all three games." />

The cost is substantial. A single agent generates 990 characters per move on average, compared with 12,000 for a homogeneous team and 14,700 for a heterogeneous team. At this setting, team deliberation is not an efficient way to systematically improve negotiation outcomes.

## Engineering

The implementation extends NegotiationArena with local open-weight inference through Hugging Face `transformers`, 8-bit quantisation for the largest models, and experiment launchers for SLURM clusters and Kaggle GPUs. A single `experiments.yaml` file defines the model groups, games, conditions, and retry settings used by the runner.

The framework records both public game histories and private inference-time traces. This makes it possible to inspect not only the move that reached the opponent, but also the drafts, critiques, discussion rounds, rankings, parser errors, and recovery attempts that produced it. The Streamlit explorer was extended to read these traces alongside the aggregate analyses.

## Conclusions

The benchmark shows that open-weight models can negotiate in all three scenarios and reproduce patterns previously reported for proprietary models, including price anchoring, positional asymmetries, and irrational rejections. They also exhibit a failure not reported for the closed-source models: sellers sometimes accept prices below their production cost. Qwen is the most consistent family across parameter tiers.

The social-persona effects do not transfer as a reliable improvement. Their value depends on the game and the model carrying the persona. Cunning in particular is high-risk: it can increase the responder's win rate while also destroying the deal and lowering mean payoff.

Self-Refine has an asymmetric effect. It helps structurally disadvantaged seats in most cells, but can hurt seats that already hold a positional advantage. The improvement is not large enough to justify the six-fold increase in generated text except, potentially, in those disadvantaged positions.

Team deliberation leaves win rate essentially unchanged and produces only localised payoff gains. Heterogeneous teams generate more diverse drafts, but the consensus mechanism selects Mistral's draft in about 80% of heterogeneous moves, despite Mistral being the weakest single family in the benchmark. The gains do not justify the twelve- to fifteen-fold inference cost.

    ["Alternative consensus mechanisms", "Compare Borda voting with majority voting, confidence-weighted voting, a representative member, or a neutral judge."],
